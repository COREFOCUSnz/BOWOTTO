Part I · The physics

What is sound, really?

Sound is not a thing that travels — it is a pattern of pressure that travels through things.

When a guitar string, a violin body, or your vocal folds vibrate, they shove the air molecules next to them. Those molecules shove their neighbours, and a ripple of squeeze-and-stretch — compression and rarefaction — races outward at about 343 m/s in air. Nothing makes the whole trip; each molecule just wobbles in place and passes the push along, like a stadium wave made of people who never leave their seats.

Your eardrum catches those pressure wobbles, and your brain decodes their rate as pitch and their size as loudness. Everything else we call tone, timbre, and texture is built from how many wobbles are stacked together and how they change over time.

Cymatics simply moves that decoding job from the ear to the eye: give the vibration a surface and something loose to push around, and the invisible pattern draws itself.

The wave machine. Faster wobbles → higher pitch. Bigger wobbles → louder sound. Doubling the frequency raises the note exactly one octave.

Frequency

hertz · Hz

How many complete vibrations happen each second. The open A string of a guitar swings 110 times a second (110 Hz); a violin's A string, 440. Humans hear roughly 20 Hz to 20,000 Hz.

Amplitude

decibels · dB

The size of each pressure swing — how hard the air is pushed. We perceive it as loudness, on a logarithmic scale: every +10 dB sounds roughly twice as loud.

Wavelength

metres · λ

The physical distance one cycle occupies in the air: speed ÷ frequency. A 20 Hz rumble is 17 metres long; a 10 kHz shimmer is 3.4 cm. This is why bass fills a room and treble beams.

Resonance

the amplifier

Every object has frequencies it "wants" to vibrate at. Feed it energy at one of those frequencies and tiny pushes build into huge motion — a wine glass ringing, a violin body blooming, a plate snapping into pattern.

Harmonics

the overtone series

A real string vibrates at its fundamental and at 2×, 3×, 4× that frequency all at once. The recipe of those overtones is timbre — why a flute and a distorted guitar playing the same note sound nothing alike.

Standing waves

the pattern maker

When a wave reflects back onto itself, the travelling motion freezes into fixed regions of wild movement (antinodes) and perfect stillness (nodes). Every cymatic figure is a map of standing-wave nodes.

Part II · Visible sound

Cymatics: four centuries of watching sound

The word comes from the Greek kyma — "wave" — but the discovery is older than the name by nearly three hundred years.

The core observation has never changed: drive a surface with a tone, and loose material on that surface migrates away from the regions that move and settles where the surface stands still. Sand bounces off the antinodes and collects on the nodes. The resulting figure is not a picture of the sound — it is a precise diagram of how that particular surface bends at that particular frequency.

Raise the pitch and the plate divides itself into smaller vibrating regions, so the pattern grows finer and more intricate. Change the plate's shape, thickness, or material and the whole family of patterns changes with it. This is exactly why a violin's carved top is tap-tuned by luthiers: they are tuning which patterns the body can make, and Chladni figures in spruce dust are still used to check the work.

On water the same physics produces something softer — rippling lattices and rosettes on the surface of a driven droplet — which is why water cymatics footage looks alive in a way sand never quite does.

  1. 1632 — Galileo Galilei

    The brass scraper

    Scraping a brass plate with a chisel, Galileo notices fine filings arranging into "a long row of fine streaks, parallel and equidistant" whenever the plate sings.

  2. 1680 — Robert Hooke

    Flour on glass

    At the Royal Society, Hooke bows the edge of a flour-dusted glass plate and watches the powder gather into figures — the first deliberate cymatics experiment.

  3. 1787 — Ernst Chladni

    The sound figures

    The "father of acoustics" systematises the trick: violin bow, metal plate, fine sand, and a published atlas of nodal patterns. His Paris demonstration so impresses Napoleon that a prize is founded for the mathematics of vibrating plates — later won by Sophie Germain.

  4. 1967 — Hans Jenny

    "Cymatics" gets its name

    The Swiss physician coins the term, drives plates and membranes with electronic oscillators, films liquids and pastes in motion, and invents the tonoscope — a device that lets the human voice draw its own patterns.

  5. Today

    Lab bench to stage

    Photographers like Alexander Lauterwasser image sound in water; engineers use the same modal analysis to design speaker cones, aircraft panels, and instruments; artists put cymatic plates on stage and let the music draw.

Appendix

Field glossary

Antinode
A point of maximum movement in a standing wave — where the sand refuses to stay.
Chladni figure
The pattern loose particles form on a vibrating plate, mapping its nodal lines.
Compression / rarefaction
The squeeze and stretch of air molecules that a sound wave is made of.
Cymatics
The study and practice of making sound and vibration visible, named by Hans Jenny (1967).
Fundamental
The lowest frequency in a sound — the one we name as its pitch.
Mode
One particular shape a surface can vibrate in; each mode has its own frequency and nodal pattern.
Node
A point (or line) of zero movement in a standing wave — where the sand collects.
Overtone
Any resonant frequency above the fundamental; whole-number multiples are called harmonics.
Resonant frequency
A frequency at which an object naturally amplifies vibration fed into it.
Sine tone
The purest possible sound: a single frequency with no overtones. The pencil of cymatics.
Standing wave
A wave pattern that oscillates in place, formed when reflections overlap the original wave.
Timbre
The character of a sound — determined by its recipe of overtones and how they evolve.
Tonoscope
Jenny's instrument for drawing patterns with the voice: a membrane, sand, and a singing tube.
Wavelength
The physical length of one wave cycle in a medium: the speed of sound divided by frequency.
