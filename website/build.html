The workshop · 4 builds

Build your own cymatics device

Cymatics is gloriously low-tech: everything here uses hardware-store parts and a phone running a free tone-generator app. Start with the salt plate — it is the classic for a reason — then work down the page.

Before you start

The three-part recipe

Every cymatics device ever made is the same three things: a driver (something that vibrates on command — a speaker, a transducer, your voice), a surface (plate, membrane, or water that the driver shakes), and a medium (salt, sand, or the water itself, free to move and settle). Change any one of the three and you get a whole new instrument. Everything below is a variation on that recipe.

For the driver signal, install a free tone generator app on your phone (search "function generator" or "tone generator" — you want one with a sine wave and a frequency slider), or use any tone-generator website on a laptop. Sine waves only: music and square waves smear several frequencies together and blur the pattern.

Build 1 · The salt plate

The classic ≈ 1 hour

Chladni's 1787 experiment with a speaker instead of a violin bow. This is the build that makes the patterns you have seen in every cymatics video.

  1. Prepare the plate. A thin metal plate 20–30 cm across is ideal — a cheap baking tray, a pizza pan, or a square of 1 mm aluminium from a hardware store. Thinner is better: it flexes more, so it patterns at lower volume.
  2. Mount the driver. Best option: a bone-conduction / surface transducer stuck to the centre of the plate's underside with its adhesive pad — it shakes the plate directly instead of the air. Budget option: rest the plate centrally on a small speaker's cone with a wine cork or a dab of adhesive putty between them.
  3. Isolate it. Sit the whole assembly on a folded towel or foam so the table doesn't steal the vibration (and your neighbours don't share the experiment).
  4. Dust the surface. Sprinkle a thin, even layer of fine table salt across the plate. Less than you think — a heavy pile dampens the plate like a hand on a cymbal.
  5. Sweep. Start the tone generator around 100 Hz at low volume and sweep upward slowly. When you cross a resonance the plate suddenly sings louder and the salt leaps — stop there, hold the frequency, and let the figure settle.
  6. Log your hits. Every plate has its own resonances. Write down the frequencies that pattern — they're your plate's personal scale, and they'll be there every time.
Troubleshooting

Salt just sits there: more volume, or a thinner plate, or a firmer bond between driver and plate. The plate must shake — put a finger on it, you should feel a strong buzz.

Salt all runs off one edge: the plate isn't level. Shim the low side.

Everything blurs and dances but never settles: you're between resonances or too loud. Back the volume off and fine-tune the frequency a few hertz either way — patterns snap in within a very narrow band.

Pattern is faint: raise the frequency; patterns get bolder and finer as modes climb. Also try sand or semolina — heavier grains draw crisper lines than salt.

Parts list

  • Thin metal plate or baking tray, 20–30 cm hardware store
  • Surface/bone-conduction transducer, 5–10 W electronics shop
  • Small amplifier or powered speaker to drive it any
  • Fine table salt or semolina kitchen
  • Adhesive putty / blu-tack stationery
  • Towel or foam pad home
  • Tone generator app free

Build 2 · The water speaker

Easiest ≈ 20 min

Hans Jenny's liquid experiments, kitchen edition. Water responds to much lower frequencies than a plate, and the patterns ripple and breathe instead of freezing.

  1. Find a sacrificial speaker. An old woofer or a cheap full-range driver, laid on its back so the cone faces the ceiling.
  2. Waterproof the cone. Line it with cling film, pressed gently into the cone's shape. (Skip this only if you truly don't care about the speaker.)
  3. Add water. About a centimetre in the bowl of the cone. A splash of milk or a drop of food colouring makes the surface easier to see.
  4. Go low. Play sine tones between 20–80 Hz. Low enough and you can watch individual ripples; around 40–60 Hz you'll get standing lattices and starburst rosettes.
  5. Light it hard. One bright lamp at a low, raking angle. The patterns live in the reflections.
  6. Film in slow motion. 240 fps on a phone turns the shimmer into visible geometry. Two tones a few hertz apart make the whole pattern slowly rotate and beat.
Troubleshooting

Water just trembles: more volume, or less water — a deep pool is heavy and slow to pattern.

Splashing everywhere: too loud. The best figures appear just below the splash threshold.

Can't see anything: it's the lighting, not the water. Kill the room lights and use one hard source at a shallow angle.

Parts list

  • Old woofer or cheap speaker driver salvage
  • Amplifier to drive it any
  • Cling film kitchen
  • Water (+ drop of milk or colouring) tap
  • Desk lamp or torch home
  • Phone with slow-motion camera pocket

Build 3 · The voice tonoscope

No electronics ≈ 30 min

Jenny's 1967 instrument rebuilt for the price of a balloon: sing into a tube, and your own voice draws the sand. The only cymatics device powered entirely by you.

  1. Make the body. A stiff cardboard tube or a length of 90–110 mm PVC pipe, 30–50 cm long. Cut one end square and clean.
  2. Stretch the membrane. Cut open a balloon (or use a square of latex sheet) and stretch it drum-tight over the squared end. Tape or a strong rubber band holds it. Tighter membrane → clearer figures.
  3. Add a mouthpiece. Sing straight into the open end, or tape a funnel or a short side-tube in for comfort — Jenny's original bent the tube so the singer could watch the membrane while singing.
  4. Dust and sing. Membrane horizontal, a pinch of fine sand or salt on top. Sing a steady vowel — ooo, ahh, eee — and hold the pitch dead flat. The sand rearranges live as you sing.
  5. Experiment. Same pitch, different vowels: different figures (vowels change the overtone recipe). Same vowel, higher pitch: finer figures. A confident singer can hold a pattern steady for as long as their breath lasts.
Troubleshooting

Nothing moves: membrane too slack, or the pitch is wandering. Tighten the latex and practise holding one note (a tuner app helps).

Sand bounces straight off: singing too loud, or membrane over-tight. This instrument likes a strong, steady mezzo-forte — not a shout.

Parts list

  • Cardboard tube or PVC pipe, 90–110 mm dia. hardware store
  • Balloon or latex sheet party shop
  • Strong rubber band or tape home
  • Fine sand or salt kitchen
  • Your voice included

Build 4 · The guitar-string strobe

Zero parts ≈ 5 min

Not a build so much as a trick — but it makes standing waves visible on an instrument you already own, and it's the fastest demo on this page.

  1. Film the low E. Hold your phone camera close over the strings and pluck the low E (82 Hz). The camera's rolling shutter samples the string line-by-line and turns the vibration into slow, ropey waves on screen.
  2. Force a node. Touch the string feather-light at the 12th fret as you pluck: you've pinned a node at the midpoint, the fundamental dies, and the octave harmonic rings with exactly half the wave shape. That touch is cymatics — you sculpted the standing wave with a fingertip.
  3. Walk the harmonics. Same trick at the 7th fret gives 3× the frequency (an octave and a fifth), the 5th fret gives 4× (two octaves). You are playing the overtone series — the same 1×, 2×, 3×, 4× ladder that gives every instrument its timbre.

Parts list

  • Guitar (any) you have one
  • Phone camera pocket
  • Bright, steady light home

Starting frequencies

Every rig is different, but these ranges are where each device wakes up. Sweep slowly — resonances are only a few hertz wide.

RangeDeviceWhat to expect
20–80 HzWater speakerVisible ripples, lattices, rotating rosettes; slow-motion territory.
80–200 HzTonoscope · low voiceBroad, simple figures — rings and crosses with thick nodal lines.
200–800 HzSalt plate · sung vowelsThe classic Chladni zone: stars, diamonds, and concentric geometry.
800 Hz–2 kHzSalt plate, pushedFine, dense, mandala-like figures; needs more volume to drive.
2 kHz +Small/stiff platesPatterns finer than the grains themselves — switch to lycopodium powder or give the mode to a smaller plate.

Filming your sessions

A producer's rig deserves producer-grade capture. Kill the room lights and use one hard source at a raking angle — patterns are relief maps, and flat light erases them. Lock focus and exposure before the sweep. For water, shoot 240 fps; for plates, normal speed but film the transition — the moment salt abandons one figure and reorganises into the next is the best footage cymatics has to offer. And record the tone to a separate track: sync the real audio back in the edit and the picture explains the sound by itself.

Protect your ears

Resonance experiments involve sustained loud tones. Keep levels moderate, take breaks, and use earplugs for long sessions — especially during sweeps, because a plate hitting resonance can jump 10–15 dB without warning. Sustained low frequencies feel quieter than they are. Hearing damage is cumulative and permanent; the patterns will wait for you. And keep water well away from mains-powered amplifiers.
